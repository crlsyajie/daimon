# The director panel

Twenty people, five per field. For each one this file records:
- **How they work**: documented habits and methods (sources at the bottom; where a detail is common
  knowledge from their filmography rather than a cited interview, it is marked *(filmography)*).
- **Checks on your work**: what that way of working makes them look for in a photo or video.
- **Rejects on sight**: the AI tells their method would never let through.

Write each note through the lens ("Through Deakins' lens: ..."), always tied to something visible in
the frame. Never invent quotes or present a note as the real person's opinion. Score 0-10; anyone whose
lens catches a FAIL gives 5 or less. In 3D/stylized mode, judge craft and hallucinations only.

## Film directors

### Stanley Kubrick
- **How he worked**: obsessive research and control; many takes until it was exactly right. On *Barry Lyndon*
  he and DP John Alcott shot candlelit interiors with no artificial boost, using NASA-developed Zeiss f/0.7
  lenses on a modified Mitchell camera, with almost no depth of field. They studied Dutch Masters, Gainsborough
  and Watteau for the look. Symmetrical one-point perspective and slow, deliberate camera moves *(filmography: 2001, The Shining)*.
- **Checks**: geometry and perspective that hold to one lens; symmetry that is exact when used; every prop placed
  on purpose; light that matches the period/source it claims (candle, window, sun); slow moves that keep straight lines straight.
- **Rejects on sight**: warped verticals, perspective that changes across the frame, random clutter that nobody
  chose, invented text, light with no source.

### David Fincher
- **How he works**: started at ILM; uses huge numbers of *invisible* effects (*The Social Network* has around 1,000 VFX
  shots) for cleanup, set extension and fixes nobody notices. Shoots many takes (often into the 40s, reports of more)
  on a locked or precisely motivated camera so the editor can split-screen the best performance from different takes.
  Camera moves only when the actor moves; no gratuitous pans.
- **Checks**: pixel-level cleanliness that still feels shot; consistency between takes and frames (hair, props,
  wardrobe, sweat, makeup); camera moves motivated by the subject; split-screen and composite seams.
- **Rejects on sight**: anything that draws attention to itself as an effect; continuity jumps; floating, unmotivated
  camera drift; skin, blood, liquids that look added.

### Denis Villeneuve
- **How he works**: favours real locations and built sets over green screen, slow reveals, silence and scale.
  With Greig Fraser on *Dune* he shot against sand-coloured screens instead of blue so the light bouncing onto actors
  matched the desert *(widely reported, see Fraser)*. Heavy atmosphere (haze, dust, fog) to give depth *(filmography: Blade Runner 2049, Sicario, Arrival)*.
- **Checks**: scale cues (people vs architecture), aerial perspective (haze increases with distance), light travelling
  through air, environment colour bouncing onto skin, pacing in video (holds long enough to feel real).
- **Rejects on sight**: clean CG air with no depth falloff, people with light colours that don't come from their
  environment, scale that doesn't add up.

### Christopher Nolan
- **How he works**: practical effects and in-camera whenever possible (crashing a real plane in *Tenet*, flipping a
  real truck in *The Dark Knight*), shooting on IMAX film, projecting space imagery outside the cockpit on *Interstellar*
  so actors react to real light *(filmography)*. Brought in physicist Kip Thorne so the black hole followed real physics.
- **Checks**: could it have been done for real? Gravity, momentum, mass, debris, contact with the ground, light from
  effects falling onto people, physically consistent trajectories (thrown objects, vehicles, water).
- **Rejects on sight**: weightless motion, missing shadows and contact, objects that don't react to forces,
  light from a VFX element that never touches the actors.

### Wong Kar-wai
- **How he works**: works without a fixed script and discovers the film while shooting and editing; long shoots;
  handheld camera with Christopher Doyle; step-printing that smears motion (*Chungking Express*); framing through
  doorways, mirrors and glass; saturated neon and humid texture *(filmography)*.
- **Checks**: is the moment caught or posed? Human imperfection in gesture, glance and timing; texture in
  surfaces and skin; intimacy; motion blur and grain that feel organic.
- **Rejects on sight**: everyone smiling at once, stock-photo staging, sterile surfaces, emotion with no specific body language.

## Cinematographers

### Roger Deakins
- **How he works**: lights so that every source has a logical origin in the scene; minimal setups, often only a few
  sources, some in frame; but not a single-source dogma (on *Jarhead* he used a grid of 45 overhead units). Says he has
  no single style; the project decides. Believes reality on screen doesn't have to be literal naturalism. Uses one LUT
  through a film rather than reinventing per shot.
- **Checks**: where is the light coming from, and does everything obey it? Shadow directions, falloff, the colour of each
  source, consistent exposure and grade between shots.
- **Rejects on sight**: light from nowhere, two suns, faces lit from a direction with no source, grade jumping shot to shot.

### Emmanuel Lubezki
- **How he works**: on *The Revenant* (with Iñárritu) the rules were chronological shooting and only natural light found
  on location; the one exception was a campfire scene with bulbs as a "cushion" of light. Used negative fill on set,
  then shaped faces vs backgrounds in the grade. Long takes, wide lenses very close to actors *(filmography: Birdman, Children of Men)*.
- **Checks**: sun, sky and bounce only; backlight and rim when the sun is behind; skin in shade lit by the colour of
  the ground and sky; long takes where light changes believably over time.
- **Rejects on sight**: front fill on a backlit subject, flat even light outdoors at golden hour, skin that ignores the environment.

### Hoyte van Hoytema
- **How he works**: shoots large format film and IMAX with Nolan; on *Oppenheimer* used IMAX black-and-white film;
  built an infrared-plus-film camera rig for day-for-night on *Ad Astra* and *Nope* *(filmography / widely reported)*.
  Cares about optical character: lens falloff, halation, grain.
- **Checks**: one consistent optical system: grain structure, highlight roll-off, halation around bright sources,
  depth of field that matches sensor size and focal length, lens distortion and vignetting.
- **Rejects on sight**: grain-free plastic areas next to crunchy sharpened ones, digital clipping in highlights,
  depth of field that doesn't match the implied lens.

### Greig Fraser
- **How he works**: pioneer of LED-volume "StageCraft" photography on *The Mandalorian* so actors and props get real
  interactive light from the environment; sand screens on *Dune*; de-tuned and custom lenses for texture
  *(widely reported / filmography)*. Integrates digital environments by capturing their light in camera.
- **Checks**: does the CG world light the people (reflections in eyes, helmets, skin colour)? Black levels and
  contrast matching between layers; consistent lens texture across real and virtual parts.
- **Rejects on sight**: green-screen spill or the wrong environment colour on skin, mismatched blacks, a background
  sharper or cleaner than the lens would render.

### Bradford Young
- **How he works**: known for underexposed, low-contrast, soft images (*Selma*, *Arrival*, *A Most Violent Year*)
  and for lighting darker skin with richness instead of over-filling it *(filmography / widely discussed in interviews)*.
  Practical light, negative fill, letting shadows hold information.
- **Checks**: honest skin tones across complexions; shadows that keep detail but stay dark; mood carried by exposure,
  not by effects.
- **Rejects on sight**: every face evenly bright, skin tones pushed to one "beauty" colour, lifted grey shadows.

## Photographers

### Annie Leibovitz
- **How she works**: concept-led portraits, heavy research into the sitter, elaborate staged productions for magazine
  covers; often one large soft key that reads like painting light, blended with ambient *(widely documented career)*.
- **Checks**: does the light shape the person and tell their story? Wardrobe, setting and pose all serving one idea;
  eye contact and expression feeling specific to that person.
- **Rejects on sight**: generic pretty light, faces with no character, staging that says nothing.

### Peter Lindbergh
- **How he worked**: hated retouching; his 2017 Pirelli calendar was a stated stand against the "terror of perfection
  and youth", showing wrinkles and real faces. Natural, often black-and-white, minimal makeup.
- **Checks**: pores, fine lines, under-eye texture, uneven skin tone, real lips and hair; makeup that looks like makeup
  on real skin.
- **Rejects on sight**: waxy, glossy, airbrushed skin; plastic smoothness; over-sharpened fake pores from an upscaler.

### Steve McCurry
- **How he works**: patient documentary work, waiting for the moment; rich colour from Kodachrome-era habits; the eyes
  as the centre (*Afghan Girl*) *(widely documented)*. His 2016 controversy over digitally altered photos shows how hard
  audiences punish manipulation in documentary images.
- **Checks**: eyes and catchlights; colour a real place would give; authentic cultural details (wardrobe, signage,
  architecture); no cloned or removed elements.
- **Rejects on sight**: dead or mismatched eyes, impossible saturation, invented cultural details, clone-stamp repeats.

### Gregory Crewdson
- **How he works**: makes single photographs like film productions: crews of roughly 40-50, months of preparation,
  sound stages or real towns, rain machines and dry ice, a cinematographer planning the light; around 50 large-format
  frames of each scene with small variations, then refined in post.
- **Checks**: staged but believable; every light placed yet motivated by a practical (lamp, window, streetlight, sun);
  story readable in one frame; the composite looks like one moment.
- **Rejects on sight**: staging without a reason, lighting that is beautiful but impossible, composite seams.

### Irving Penn
- **How he worked**: austere studio setups (the 1948 corner portraits between two angled flats), bare backdrops, mostly
  window or north light even when travelling with a portable daylight studio; meticulous still life and product work.
- **Checks**: material truth of objects and products: edges, surface texture, reflections, labels and seams; simple,
  controlled light that reveals form.
- **Rejects on sight**: wrong product details (logos, stitching, seams, panel layouts), objects with mushy edges,
  surfaces that don't reflect their environment.

## VFX / CGI supervisors

### Joe Letteri (Weta FX)
- **How he works**: co-developed the subsurface-scattering skin for Gollum (Academy technical award) because translucency
  is what makes skin and eyes believable; digital humans and creatures from *The Two Towers* to *Avatar*.
- **Checks**: light passing through skin (ears, nostrils, fingers in backlight), eyes (wetness, refraction, catchlights),
  anatomy and gait of people and animals, fur and hair strand detail.
- **Rejects on sight**: skin that reflects light like plastic, dead eyes, animals with wrong anatomy or painted fur.

### Paul Franklin (DNEG)
- **How he works**: on *Interstellar* turned Kip Thorne's general-relativity equations into the renderer for the black hole,
  then made controlled, deliberate changes for visibility. Physically based light and scale first, art second.
- **Checks**: light transport (shadows, bounce, reflections) all physically consistent; scale and distance cues;
  simulations (water, smoke, dust, cloth) behaving at the right size and speed.
- **Rejects on sight**: physically impossible light, miniature-looking scale, simulations moving at the wrong speed.

### John Knoll (ILM)
- **How he works**: long-time ILM visual effects supervisor (*Star Wars* prequels, *Pirates of the Caribbean*, *Rogue One*),
  and co-creator of Photoshop *(widely documented)*. Compositing and integration discipline.
- **Checks**: edges (no cut-out sharpness, correct light wrap), matching grain, black levels, colour and focus between
  layers, haze over distant elements, reflections of the comp in the scene.
- **Rejects on sight**: halos, hard mattes, layers with different grain or sharpness, missing light wrap.

### Rob Legato
- **How he works**: virtual production on *The Jungle Book* and *The Lion King*: a virtual camera that models a real ARRI
  Alexa (lens distortion, depth of field, flares) and is operated like real gear (dollies, cranes, fluid heads) so CG
  feels photographed; welcomes "happy accidents" in performance.
- **Checks**: camera language: could an operator with real equipment have made this move? Focus pulls, lens
  breathing, operator imperfection, flare behaviour.
- **Rejects on sight**: impossible weightless camera paths, perfect robotic moves, focus that never misses, flares that ignore the lens.

### Dennis Muren (ILM)
- **How he works**: ILM's most awarded VFX supervisor (*Jurassic Park*, *Terminator 2*); championed the CG dinosaurs over
  stop-motion once the test looked real enough (Letteri was on that test) *(widely documented)*. Judges by whether the shot
  feels photographed.
- **Checks**: the gut reaction at first glance and at full size; what breaks the illusion first; the frame as a whole, not just the details.
- **Rejects on sight**: anything that makes you feel "something is off" before you can explain it. Find out what it is and name it.
